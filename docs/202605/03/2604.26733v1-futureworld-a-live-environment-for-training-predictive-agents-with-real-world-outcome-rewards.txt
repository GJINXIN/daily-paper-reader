Abstract 

Live future prediction refers to the task of making predictions about real-world events before they unfold. This task is increasingly studied using large language model-based agent systems, and it is important for building agents that can con-tinually learn from real-world. Just as interactive environments have often driven progress in agents, advancing live future prediction naturally motivates viewing it as a learning environment. Prior works have explored future prediction from several different parts, but have generally not framed it as a unified learning envi-ronment. This task is appealing for learning because it can provide a large number of prediction questions grounded in diverse real-world events, while preventing answer leakage. To leverage the advantages of live future prediction, we present FutureWorld, a live agentic reinforcement learning environment that closes the training loop between prediction, outcome realization, and parameters update. In our environment, we take three open-source base models and train them for con-secutive days. The results show that training is effective. Furthermore, we build a daily benchmark based on the environment and evaluate several frontier agents on it to establish performance baselines for current agent systems. 

1 Introduction 

Live future prediction refers to the task of making predictions about real-world events before they unfold, where the results are unknown at prediction time but can be verified based on subsequently observed real-world outcomes Zeng et al. [2025], Liu et al. [2026], Karger et al. [2025]. Recent works Zeng et al. [2025], Liu et al. [2026], Karger et al. [2025], Turtel et al. [2025], Metaculus [2025], UniPat AI [2026], Jeen et al. [2026], Su et al. [2026] increasingly study this task using large language model (LLM)-based agent systems, often in the form of agentic prediction pipelines that retrieve relevant information, reason over retrieved evidence, and generate predictions. This task naturally supports the development of agent systems that make predictions, observe realized outcomes, and update their behavior accordingly. In this way, they can remain aligned with evolving real-world dynamics instead of relying solely on static knowledge acquired during training. This ability is important for building agents that can continually learn from real-world feedback, adapt to changing conditions, and remain reliable in dynamic real-world environments.

Recent works Zhou et al. [2024], Trivedi et al. [2024], Drouin et al. [2024], Majumder et al. [2023], Chen et al. [2025] on LLM agents have often advanced capability development through interactive environments. In the same spirit, improving live future prediction also requires prediction, outcome feedback, and policy improvement over time, which naturally motivates treating it as a learning environment. Such an environment should satisfy three requirements. First, the stream of questions should remain live, because new prediction targets continually emerge from ongoing real-world developments, and a static collection of historical questions cannot meet the need for live continual learning. Second, the learning signal should be grounded in realized outcomes, rather than relying solely on proxy signals such as process rewards, so that optimization remains aligned with the true objective of prediction. Third, training should include the agent’s own information retrieval and evidence analysis, because in realistic future prediction the agent must learn not only how to make predictions from evidence, but also what evidence to seek and how to interpret it. In this way, live future prediction defines a learning environment in which agents can continually adapt their policies through real-world feedback and continually update their understanding of an evolving world. Prior works have explored future prediction from several different parts. Existing works Zeng et al. [2025], Karger et al. [2025], Liu et al. [2026] take an important step by providing live evaluation infrastructures that continually introduce new prediction questions over time. Another step is to place learning and agentic rollouts inside a live setting. UniPat AI [2026] moves in this direction by combining reinforcement learning (RL) with agentic rollouts in a live prediction setup. However, its learning signal is based on rubric-based process rewards rather than realized outcomes, leaving a gap between the training signal and the true objective of prediction. A further step is to optimize learning directly against realized outcomes. Recent works Turtel et al. [2025], Jeen et al. [2026] move in this objective by exploring outcome-based RL. However, they do so only on static datasets of previously resolved questions, where the evidence available for prediction is fixed in advance. As a result, the agent is not truly trained to search for, select, and interpret evidence in a live environment. Taken together, prior work has explored different important aspects of live future prediction, but no existing work has yet established it as a unified training environment. A closer look at live future prediction reveals several properties that make it particularly well suited as a learning environment. Feedback can be derived directly from real-world outcomes, without requiring human annotation. This greatly reduces supervision cost and makes it possible to scale training over a large number of prediction questions. In addition, because the real world is rich and diverse, prediction questions can naturally span a wide range of domains. Notably, data leakage is prevented by construction. These properties make live future prediction particularly well suited as a learning environment. To leverage the advantages of live future prediction, we present FutureWorld, a live agentic RL environment that closes the training loop between prediction, outcome realization, and parameters update. Each day, the system automatically generates a large number (2047.29 on average, see Section 3.5) of prediction questions from a broad set of carefully selected, high-value event sources spanning many sectors, as illustrated in Figure 1 (a). The generated questions are first filtered to remove low-quality items, after which the remaining questions are resampled to balance question counts across domains. For each prediction question, the agent executes a rollout that may involve issuing search queries, reading retrieved documents, reasoning over evidence, and producing a prediction, while the environment records the resulting trajectory. The ground-truth outcome is provided only later, when the event resolves, at which point it is matched to the corresponding stored rollout and used to compute a reward. Many accumulated rewards are then used to update the model’s parameters. The entire pipeline runs autonomously on a daily cycle and covers question generation, rollout execution, outcome collection, reward computation, and model update. In our environment, we take three open-source base models and train them using outcome-based RL. The results show that training is effective. Furthermore, we build a daily benchmark based on the environment and evaluate several frontier agents on it to establish performance baselines for current agent systems. 2 Related work 

2.1 Agent environments 

Agent environments have established the value of interaction and feedback for training and evaluating large language agents. Prior works have studied a range of controlled environments for sequential de-cision making. Early text-based settings such as Jericho Hausknecht et al. [2020] provide interactive fiction games for language-conditioned action, ScienceWorld Wang et al. [2022] focuses on grounded scientific reasoning in an interactive text environment, and ALFWorld Shridhar et al. [2021] connects text-based interaction with embodied household tasks. Other work studies web and software interac-tion in more realistic yet still controllable settings. WebShop Yao et al. [2023] formulates grounded web interaction as an online shopping task, WebArena Zhou et al. [2024] recreates functional websites in a reproducible sandbox, VisualWebArena Koh et al. [2024] extends this paradigm to visually grounded web tasks, AppWorld Trivedi et al. [2024] builds a controllable ecosystem of apps populated with fictitious users, and WorkArena Drouin et al. [2024] targets browser-based knowledge-work tasks in enterprise software. However, despite their diversity, most of these environments remain closed or semi-closed, which limit their ability to expose agents to open-ended real-world change. 

2.2 Benchmarks for future prediction 

Future prediction benchmarks bring evaluation closer to real-world outcomes that unfold over time, yet they remain primarily designed for assessment rather than training. Earlier benchmarks such as ForecastQA Jin et al. [2021] and AutoCast Zou et al. [2022] formulate prediction with temporal text or historical prediction questions, while PROPHET Tao et al. [2025] further emphasizes inferability by constructing prediction questions paired with supporting news evidence. More recent efforts have moved prediction evaluation closer to live settings. ForecastBench Karger et al. [2025] and FutureX Zeng et al. [2025] introduce automatically updated benchmarks of unresolved prediction questions, while FutureX-Pro Liu et al. [2026] extends this live paradigm to higher-value vertical domains.

3 FutureWorld environment 

3.1 Environment overview 

FutureWorld is a live environment for future prediction that supports the complete pipeline from question generation to delayed reward assignment. As illustrated in Figure 2, the process begins by collecting data from a broad set of public online sources about candidate future events, and then constructs question-description pairs by instantiating prediction questions through predefined rules or templates and, when applicable, associating them with supplementary descriptions that provide additional background context. These pairs are further processed through filtering and resampling so that the final retained set is both higher quality and more balanced across domains. The retained questions are then transformed, via predefined prompt templates, into probability-estimation prompts that ask the agent to estimate the probability that the specific future event will actually occur. Once deployed in the environment, as shown in Figure 3, an agent interacts with these prompts by conducting search and reasoning before producing a probabilistic prediction. FutureWorld records each prediction episode as a rollout trajectory. At later scheduled times, when the corresponding real-world outcomes are expected to be available according to their original resolution schedule, the environment attempts to retrieve the ground truth, injects resolved outcomes back into the

3.2 Data Sources 

FutureWorld begins by collecting candidate future events from a broad set of online sources through automated network requests. 4 In the current version, we maintain a pool of 72 candidate websites. 5

These sources cover a wide range of domains, enabling the environment to generate prediction tasks with substantial breadth. Figure 1 (a) shows the detailed categorization of the website sources and the proportion accounted for by each domain. Moreover, most of these website sources track more consequential real-world developments. 

3.3 Construction of question-description pairs 

After collecting data from source websites, FutureWorld instantiates candidate prediction questions using predefined rules or templates. These questions are formulated as binary prediction questions that ask whether a specific event will occur. For example, a question may take the form Will the highest temperature in Dallas be between 84-85°F on April 18? Each question may also be paired with a supplementary description that is not a necessary part of the question itself, but instead provides additional context to make the prediction target more explicit and provide more complete background information. The question alone remains sufficient for answering, while the description can provide richer background information. For some websites, the description can be directly extracted from the raw source data, whereas for others, we use descriptions that have been pre-generated with the assistance of an LLM, i.e., GPT-5.4 6. In this way, FutureWorld constructs question-description pairs that are used in the subsequent filtering (Section 3.4) and resampling (Section 3.5) stages. 7

63.6 Prompt construction for agent prediction 

After resampling, FutureWorld obtains the final set of retained prediction questions and applies a predefined prompt template to transform them into executable future prediction prompts that can be directly provided to LLM agents. Although the retained questions are all binary questions, the template reformulates them as probability estimation tasks by asking the agent to estimate the likelihood that each event will occur. This reformulation allows the agent to express uncertainty explicitly and yields a richer, more fine-grained supervision signal for learning. Although some questions are paired with supplementary descriptions in earlier stages to support filtering and resampling, these descriptions are intentionally omitted from the final prompts given to the agent, so that the task remains appropriately challenging and the agent is not given additional hints. 

3.7 Agent prediction and trajectory logging 

For each prediction question, FutureWorld creates an agent episode in which the model receives an executable prediction prompt and produces a probability estimate before the corresponding real-world outcome is known. In our training setup, the agent is required to perform at least one web-search action before giving its final prediction, and it may conduct multiple rounds of search and information analysis if needed. The final response must therefore be a probability in [0 , 1] , while non-probabilistic outputs are treated as invalid for training. FutureWorld records a complete prediction episode as a rollout trajectory. This stored trajectory includes the prompt, the sequence of model-generated actions, the environment observations returned after valid tool calls, such as retrieved search results and information snippets, and the final model response. In practice, multiple stochastic rollouts may be sampled for the same question. As a result, the logged trajectory serves as a fixed behavioral record of the agent’s decision process at prediction time, which is essential for learning from delayed real-world feedback. 

3.8 Ground-truth retrieval and learning from delayed rewards 

At regular intervals, FutureWorld attempts to retrieve the ground-truth outcomes of previously issued prediction questions. In the replay phase, the resolved outcomes are injected back into the stored prediction episodes and used to compute outcome-based rewards according to a predefined function. Questions that remain unresolved at a scheduled replay time are either left pending until later resolution or discarded from reward computation. The effective replayed trajectories are then used for RL updates, allowing the model to improve the search and reasoning behavior that produced the prediction. 

4 Training Agents in FutureWorld 

4.1 Daily schedule strategy 

After constructing FutureWorld as a learning environment, we train three agents based on 

Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 Team [2025], Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct Team [2024], Yang et al. [2024], and DeepSeek-R1-0528-Qwen3-8B DeepSeek-AI [2025], respectively. To maintain a high-frequency update cycle, the environment runs on a daily schedule. Specifically, at 20:00 each evening, the environment releases a batch of prediction questions whose outcomes are expected to be resolved on the following day, while simultaneously retrieving the ground-truth outcomes for the batch released on the previous evening. We fix the number of prediction questions released each day at 500. Real-world events and public reporting are not perfectly synchronized. As a result, ground truth cannot always be retrieved on time. Even when an event has already resolved, its outcome may not yet be available on the corresponding source website because of delays in data collection, statistical processing, or public release. This may occur for government or public-interest data sources, where published records can lag behind the underlying event by one or more days. Moreover, some prediction targets may be postponed or canceled. Therefore, when ground-truth retrieval is performed on the evening, some questions may still remain unresolved. Across five consecutive days 7of observation, an average of 35.65% of prediction questions do not have retrievable ground truth at the scheduled retrieval time. When ground-truth retrieval is performed not on the evening of the expected resolution day, but on the following evening instead, the proportion of questions that remain unresolved decreased to 20%. When ground truth cannot be successfully retrieved, our strategy is to discard corresponding questions and exclude them from computing rewards. This choice is simple and practical from an implemen-tation perspective, but it inevitably wastes both computation and potentially valuable supervision signal, because some of these discarded questions may become resolvable after a longer delay. How to handle such longer delayed feedback more effectively, remains an important direction for our future work. 

4.2 Rollout generation 

In addition to the prediction prompt prepared by FutureWorld, the system prompt also instructs the agent to issue at least one Google search action. The agent is given a lightweight Google-search interface backed by the Serper API 10 . Tool observations are provided as context for the agent’s subsequent reasoning, while being masked out from the delayed policy loss. For each question, we sample four stochastic trajectories with temperature 1.0 and top-p = 1 .0,meaning that nucleus sampling Holtzman et al. [2020] is disabled. Each trajectory records the full interaction history, including the original prompt, every search action generated by the agent, the corresponding observations returned by the search tool, and the final probability response.

4.4 Experiments 4.4.1 Implementation details 

To decouple data collection, inference, answer acquisition, and model updating, we modify verl-tool [Jiang et al., 2025b] to support storing the contents of LLM rollouts on local disk. We refer to our modified framework as verl-tool-future , which we plan to release as open source in the future. All experiments are conducted on 2 Nvidia A100 GPUs. We train the policy with Verl-Tool Jiang et al. [2025a], using FSDP Zhao et al. [2023] as the distributed training backend and vLLM Kwon et al. [2023] for rollout generation. 5 FutureWorld daily benchmark 

5.1 Benchmark design 

Beyond its role as a training environment, FutureWorld also supports a live benchmark for evaluating state-of-the-art agents on future prediction. This benchmark is constructed from the data collected by our environment. Compared with the dataset used for RL training, it features both greater diversity in question formats and higher overall difficulty. For practical cost reasons, we limit the benchmark to at most 50 live prediction questions per day. We release a subset of our historical data on Hugging Face 12 .The benchmark contains four question types. The first is binary choice questions, each with exactly one correct option, with no more than 5 such questions per day. The second is simple multiple choice questions, where there may be one or multiple correct options and the number of options is 3 or 4, with no more than 10 such questions per day. The third is difficult multiple choice questions, where there may be one or multiple correct options and the total number of options is greater than 4 but no more than 26, with no more than 15 such questions per day. The fourth is numeric prediction questions, which ask the model to predict a specific value, with no more than 20 such questions per day. Table 1 provides representative examples for each question type. Each day at 20:00, the benchmark is refreshed with a new batch of prediction questions whose answers are expected to be revealed on the following day. Because the benchmark is used only for evaluation, we allow a longer delay before answer collection. Specifically, we retrieve the ground-truth outcomes for the batch of questions released two evenings earlier. This design increases the fraction of questions that can be resolved. Since many real-world events and information sources are governed by recurring weekly and holiday-related schedules, the benchmark may exhibit periodic distribution shifts. For instance, some types of information are unlikely to be released when markets are closed or public institutions are not operating, while other events such as sports competitions or entertainment programs are more likely to occur on weekends. Consequently, the composition of benchmark questions can shift abruptly across some calendar boundaries. For evaluation, we use different scoring rules for different question types. 13 6 Conclusion 

We introduce FutureWorld, a live environment for training predictive agents with real-world outcome rewards. Unlike prior works that rely on static collections of resolved questions or proxy process-based rewards, FutureWorld keeps the prediction stream live, places agentic search and reasoning inside the training loop, and learns directly from realized future outcomes. Our experiments show that this delayed real-world feedback can serve as an effective training signal, leading to improved prediction performance over successive days, while the benchmark built on top of FutureWorld also provides a practical testbed for evaluating frontier agents on live prediction tasks. We hope FutureWorld can serve as a useful step toward agent systems that continually improve by making predictions, observing outcomes, and adapting to an evolving real world.
