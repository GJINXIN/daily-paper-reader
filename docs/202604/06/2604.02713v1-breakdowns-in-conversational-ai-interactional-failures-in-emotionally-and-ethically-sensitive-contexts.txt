## Abstract 

Conversational AI is increasingly deployed in emotionally charged and ethically sensitive interactions. Previous research has primar-ily concentrated on emotional benchmarks or static safety checks, overlooking how alignment unfolds in evolving conversation. We explore the research question: what breakdowns arise when con-versational agents confront emotionally and ethically sensitive behaviors, and how do these affect dialogue quality? To stress-test chatbot performance, we develop a persona-conditioned user simu-lator capable of engaging in multi-turn dialogue with psychological personas and staged emotional pacing. Our analysis reveals that mainstream models exhibit recurrent breakdowns that intensify as emotional trajectories escalate. We identify several common fail-ure patterns, including affective misalignments, ethical guidance failures, and cross-dimensional trade-offs where empathy super-sedes or undermines responsibility. We organize these patterns into a taxonomy and discuss the design implications, highlighting the necessity to maintain ethical coherence and affective sensi-tivity throughout dynamic interactions. The study offers the HCI community a new perspective on the diagnosis and improvement of conversational AI in value-sensitive and emotionally charged contexts.

## 1 Introduction 

Conversational AI has rapidly evolved from research prototypes to widespread use, mediating activities ranging from customer service and information search to friendship and assistance [ 32 , 36 ]. As these systems grow more integrated into daily life, users’ expecta-tions expand beyond factual accuracy and efficiency: they increas-ingly face them at moments of vulnerability, doubt, or moral strain. Conversations in such environments are not only informative but also emotionally and ethically delicate [ 22 , 31 , 34 ]. The system’s response can influence whether users feel validated, whether un-pleasant ideas are supported or challenged, and whether trust in AI-mediated interactions is maintained. These situations are impor-tant to the HCI community because they emphasize alignment as a lived experience, where faults emerge not just as content problems, but also as breakdowns in empathy, accountability, and ethical coherence. Previous research has made significant advances toward safer and more socially aware conversational bots. On one hand, align-ment approaches such as reinforcement learning from human feed-back (RLHF) and constitutional AI significantly decrease overt harms by restricting responses to ethically acceptable bounds [ 3 , 25 ]. Affective computing research, on the other hand, has focused on empathetic response generation and prosocial dialogue datasets [16 ], with the goal of making systems more helpful in emotionally vulnerable circumstances. However, these two paths have mainly run parallel: safety-oriented models frequently reply with inflexi-ble refusals that create emotional gaps [ 28 , 40 ], whereas empathy-oriented systems may mistakenly collaborate with damaging ratio-nalization or dissolve moral limits [ 34 ]. Furthermore, most evalu-ations focus on static or single-turn prompts, leaving unresolved the question of how conversational agents perform when commu-nication progresses through turns [10]. Recent frameworks for emotional-support evaluation and ethical-risk assessment [ 4 , 13 , 23 , 47 ] address important aspects of safety or empathy, yet they primarily employ single-turn or lightly scaffolded multi-turn setups and do not model persona-grounded, escalating interaction trajectories where ethical and affective tensions evolve dynamically. As a result, it is uncertain whether present systems can preserve emotional attunement and ethical coherence in multi-turn

interaction, especially under conditions of escalation and moral disengagement. We argue that multi-turn emotionally sensitive dia-logue uniquely exposes interactional tensions in alignment, where attempts to be empathetic can inadvertently weaken moral bound-aries, and attempts to enforce responsibility can disrupt relational attunement. This paper bridges that gap by posing a research question: What breakdowns occur when conversational AI interacts with emotionally and ethically sensitive user behaviors, and how do these breakdowns affect dialogue quality? To investigate, we simulate multi-turn con-versations between persona-conditioned users and popular chatbot models. To anchor these simulations in practical settings, we adapt scenarios from ProsocialDialogue [16 ] and use staged emotional pacing to simulate escalation dynamics, supplemented with quanti-tative checks of persona and pacing validity. We then assess chatbot responses using a combination of LLM-as-judge evaluations and automated diversity indicators, supported by a small-scale human validation of judge reliability, allowing for a systematic interactional investigation of how alignment is maintained or compromised in sensitive conversational contexts. According to our evaluation, we find that advanced conversa-tional agents generally maintain a safe and polite tone, but struggle to balance empathy with ethical guidance as interactions evolve. These tensions manifest as shallow or repetitive responses, rigid moralizing, or diluted boundary-setting, particularly in ethically sensitive or ambiguous scenarios. To examine these breakdowns in depth, we analyze the failure cases and consolidate recurrent pat-terns into a taxonomy comprising three major categories ( affective misalignments, ethical guidance failures, and cross-dimensional trade-offs ), covering seven fine-grained breakdown subtypes. Additional analysis of breakdown distributions shows that these failures recur across personas and scenarios, and become more likely as emotional trajectories escalate. Overall, these findings indicate that the fail-ures are not random events, but rather systematic problems caused by structural tensions in the current alignment methods. Building on this taxonomy, we discuss the causes and consequences of these breakdowns, as well as the design implications for more resilient conversational agents. Our main contributions are as follows: 

• We propose an interactional evaluation approach that lever-ages a persona-conditioned simulator with staged emotion pacing to probe conversational agents in emotionally and ethically sensitive contexts. 

• Our persona-conditioned simulations reveal that mainstream chatbots struggle to balance empathy and ethical guidance across multi-turn conversations, with breakdowns intensify-ing as emotional tension escalates. 

• We further analyze these failures, compile them into a taxon-omy of recurrent breakdown modes, and derive design im-plications, emphasizing the importance of jointly calibrating empathy and responsibility in future alignment strategies. 

## 2 Related Work 2.1 Empathy and Emotional Support in Conversational Agents 

A longstanding strand of HCI and NLP research has investigated how conversational agents can provide empathy and emotional support. Early systems such as Woebot [ 9 ] and Replika [ 29 ] demon-strated that lightweight, scripted chatbots could offer accessible mental health support by simulating empathetic listening. Subse-quent datasets such as EmpatheticDialogues [ 31 ] and emotional support corpora [ 22 ] fueled the development of deep learning mod-els trained explicitly for empathetic response generation. Large-scale dialogue models such as BlenderBot [ 32 ] and DialoGPT [ 44 ]further incorporated affective signals into end-to-end architectures. Recent advances with large language models (LLMs) have ex-panded the scope of emotionally supportive agents [ 5, 42 ]. However, emerging work highlights substantial psychological and ethical risks in emotionally charged or counseling-like settings. Moore et al . [24] demonstrate that advanced LLMs continue to exhibit inappropriate or stigmatizing responses in mental health contexts, whereas Chandra et al . [4] identify nineteen recurring AI behaviors linked to adverse mental health outcomes. Longitudinal evidence indicates downstream effects on users: a four-week randomized con-trolled trial [ 8 ] links intensive chatbot use with increased loneliness and dependency. Ethical analyses provide additional documentation of violations, including boundary crossing, misplaced validation, and breaches of confidentiality [ 13 ]. Culturally aligned emotional support continues to pose challenges. Liu et al . [20] show that sim-plistic role-playing of cultural personas does not result in authentic cultural sensitivity. Together, these works reveal that empathy in LLMs extends beyond affective resonance, and it must be balanced against eth-ical responsibility. Yet most prior research examines single-turn responses, aggregate risk categories, or user outcomes, neglecting the analysis of how emotional and ethical tensions accumulate over multiple turns. Our work addresses this gap by investigating in-teractional breakdowns that emerge in multi-turn, emotionally and ethically charged conversations. 

## 2.2 Ethical and Safety Alignment in Dialogue Systems 

Alongside efforts to enhance empathy, a parallel body of work ex-amines how conversational agents remain safe and aligned with human values. In the NLP community, alignment techniques such as reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) [ 2, 25 ] and instruction tuning [ 39 ] have become the de facto standard for shap-ing LLM behavior. These methods prioritize risk-averse responses by training models to avoid harmful, biased, or offensive outputs. Yet red-teaming studies show that even highly aligned systems continue to exhibit failure modes when probed with adversarial or ambiguous prompts [30, 45]. HCI research further reveals how alignment strategies play out in real-world interaction. Work on refusal and denial styles [ 41 ]shows that aggressive risk-avoidance can frustrate users or erode perceptions of transparency. Other studies highlight tensions be-tween warmth, authenticity, and safety. Seitz [34] report that em-pathetic or sympathetic responses may increase perceived warmth but decrease perceived sincerity, while Pasch [28] show that safety-motivated refusals depress satisfaction when the model’s stance is not contextually aligned. These findings indicate that alignment is not purely a static property of a system, but something that re-quires dynamic maintenance throughout the evolution of dialogue. This view is supported by recent analyses of social alignment fail-ures. Cheng et al . [6] document how models frequently over-agree with users in order to maintain social harmony, endorsing harmful or morally problematic statements as a form of politeness-driven conformity. Conversely, cultural alignment studies [ 20 ] show that genuine attunement requires attention to culturally situated norms rather than surface-level imitation. These strands collectively point to an emerging insight: align-ment is dynamic, socially situated, and vulnerable to breakdown when emotional and ethical demands conflict. While prior work identifies failure modes in isolation, far fewer studies examine how such tensions unfold interactionally—across turns, within trajecto-ries, and under evolving affective pressure. Our analysis contributes to this gap by tracing how empathy, responsibility, and moral stance collide and shift during multi-turn exchanges. 

## 2.3 Evaluation and Simulation Approaches for Conversational AI 

Evaluation has long been a central challenge for conversational AI. Early automatic metrics such as BLEU, ROUGE, and perplexity [ 19 , 21 , 27 ] fall short of capturing coherence, empathy, or appro-priateness in open-ended dialogue. Later approaches incorporated diversity metrics [ 17 , 43 ] or user-centered affective ratings [ 31 ], but these remain limited to surface-level qualities. The advent of LLM-based evaluation has renewed interest in scalable and struc-tured assessments. Systems such as MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena [ 48 ] or JudgeLM [ 49 ] employ LLMs as judges and achieve high agreement with human preferences. However, these frameworks typically evaluate isolated turns or short segments, limiting visibil-ity into how conversational quality changes over the course of an unfolding interaction. Simulation methods provide an alternative lens for probing dia-logue behavior. Classic user simulators in task-oriented dialogue [ 18 , 33 ] inspired role-based and persona-based agents for coun-seling and emotional support. Recent frameworks in this space extend multi-turn simulation to more psychologically grounded scenarios. ESC-Eval [ 47 ] generates distressed role-play users to assess empathic reasoning, while ESC-Judge [ 23 ] evaluates coun-seling conversations through an Exploration-Insight-Action rubric. More interactive systems such as Ψ-Arena [ 50 ] incorporate tri-adic feedback loops and psychologically profiled clients to optimize LLM counselors. Other frameworks assess socio-emotional capacity more abstractly. BOLT [ 7 ] links behavioral patterns to therapeutic orientations, EQ-Bench [ 26 ] evaluates emotional intelligence up-stream of conversation, and SPHERE [ 46 ] provides standards for ensuring methodological transparency and evaluation validity. Although these efforts broaden multi-turn evaluation, they share two assumptions that limit their coverage of alignment dynamics. First, they usually evaluate systems interacting with cooperative or support-seeking users, emphasizing capacities such as reflective listening, phase transitions, or helpfulness. Second, user trajectories are generally scenario-based or scripted, rather than being dynam-ically influenced by emotional escalation, moral disengagement, or adversarial reasoning. Consequently, current simulators offer limited visibility into how conversational agents respond when em-pathy and ethics come into tension, when users repeatedly justify harmful behavior, or when alignment gradually drifts across turns. Our work extends this line of research by focusing on how align-ment changes as a conversation unfolds, particularly in emotionally and ethically charged settings. We develop a persona-conditioned simulator that integrates psychologically grounded user profiles with a staged emotional-escalation model to generate controlled, reproducible trajectories. Rather than evaluating isolated conversa-tional skills, we investigate whether chatbots can maintain consis-tent ethical positions and attuned emotional responses throughout interactions, highlighting breakdowns that are challenging to iden-tify using current evaluation or simulation techniques. 

## 3 Persona-Conditioned Simulation 

Our goal is to empirically explore how mainstream conversational agents respond to emotionally sensitive and ethically complex inter-actions. To this end, we design a persona-conditioned user simulator 

that generates adversarial yet psychologically coherent user behav-iors in multi-turn dialogues. Its purpose is not to replicate real users in full fidelity, but to provide a controlled and theory-informed input source for evaluating conversational agents. To operationalize this idea, we build a simulation pipeline with two components: (1) per-sona construction , which builds structured psychological profiles from ethically salient dialogues, and (2) multi-turn simulation ,which generates persona-grounded user utterances modulated by an emotion pacing function. As shown in Figure 1, these compo-nents enable us to systematically probe whether conversational agents break down under escalating emotional trajectories. 

## 3.1 Persona Profile Construction 

We construct structured persona profiles to anchor simulated user behavior in stable psychological and ethical stances across multi-turn interactions. Rather than modeling users as arbitrary adver-saries, personas encode persistent values, affective orientations, and responsibility negotiations that remain consistent across turns. 

3.1.1 Persona Schema. To represent the psychologically coher-ent stance that users adopt in ethically sensitive interactions, we construct structured persona profiles that encode affective, motiva-tional, and value-oriented cues observable in dialogue. Prior work in moral cognition and interpersonal communication shows that breakdowns in sensitive conversations often arise when emotional orientation, responsibility negotiation, or relational stance become misaligned [ 34 , 35 ]. These cues are explicitly expressed in user utterances, making them suitable for structured extraction. 3.1.2 Persona Extraction and Validation. Persona profiles are ex-tracted from ethically sensitive dialogues using a large language model, grounded strictly in observable user utterances. To ensure coverage of diverse moral and emotional stressors, we draw can-didate scenarios from the ProsocialDialog (ProS.) dataset [ 16 ] and categorize them into six ethically salient scenario types: (1) serious illegal acts, (2) ethical violations, (3) moral dilemmas, (4) social misconduct, (5) potentially harmful cases, and (6) benign conversa-tions. These categories are used solely to support stratified sampling rather than as ground-truth semantic labels. Using this process, we curate a set of 298 dialogues spanning a wide range of ethically complex interaction contexts. Each di-alogue is then processed to infer a structured persona following the predefined schema, yielding profiles that capture stable affec-tive and normative stances used to condition subsequent simula-tion. To assess whether extracted personas remain grounded in the source dialogues, we conduct a lightweight human validation study. Across persona dimensions, annotators report high groundedness and moderate-to-strong agreement, indicating that the extraction process produces interpretable and reliable representations suitable for downstream simulation and analysis. Further details on scenario selection, persona extraction, and human validation procedures are provided in Appendix A. 

## 3.2 Multi-turn User Simulation 

To stress-test conversational agents under emotionally and ethically sensitive conditions, we construct a multi-turn user simulator that generates adversarial yet psychologically coherent dialogue trajec-tories. The simulator models how users naturally escalate, rational-ize, or deflect responsibility across turns, that static or single-turn adversarial prompts often fail to capture. Our simulation relies on three key elements: a structured persona profile (Sec. 3.1) ground-ing user stance, a turn-level emotion pacing function (Sec. 3.2.1) regulating affective trajectory, and a controlled response genera-tion mechanism (Sec. 3.2.2). These components yield controlled yet diverse behavioral trajectories for systematic cross-model eval-uation. 

3.2.1 Emotion Pacing Function. Our pilot studies showed that persona-only simulations often produced unrealistic dynamics: some users escalated to hostility immediately, while others re-mained emotionally flat. To approximate the more gradual unfold-ing of real conversations, we introduce an emotion pacing function

abstraction to represent turn progression, where each turn is asso-ciated with a rhetorical target determined by dialogue phase. For a dialogue of length 𝑇 , we compute a normalized phase 𝜙 𝑡 = 𝑡 /𝑇 ,and map it to one of five escalating strategies 𝑒 𝑡 = 𝜋 (𝜙 𝑡 ).

These phases reflect common escalation patterns observed in pilot simulations, abstracting away from overly abrupt or flat dy-namics without prescribing exhaustive models of user behavior. By introducing paced escalation, the simulator captures more gradual and psychologically plausible interaction trajectories, enabling anal-ysis of whether conversational agents break down under ethical and emotional stress. 

3.2.2 Response Generation. At each turn 𝑡 , the simulator generates a user utterance 𝑢 𝑡 conditioned on three inputs: the persona profile 

𝑃 , dialogue history 𝐻 <𝑡 , and rhetorical target 𝑒 𝑡 from the emotion pacing function: 𝑢 𝑡 = LM_Generate (𝐻 <𝑡 , 𝑃, 𝑒 𝑡 ). The persona pro-file provides internal consistency, the pacing function specifies the expected rhetorical move at that stage, and the dialogue history grounds responses in prior turns. To ensure progressive dynamics, each prompt explicitly encodes the turn number and the corre-sponding strategy (Table 11 in Appendix B). This design enables the simulator to generate adversarial yet psychologically coherent user behaviors that escalate in staged, value-sensitive steps.

This section reports our empirical evaluation of how mainstream chatbots behave in ethically challenging and emotionally charged conversations. We stress-test models using a persona-conditioned simulator across diverse ethical scenarios, and evaluate their re-sponses along tone, empathy, ethical guidance, and engagement.

4.1.2 Evaluation Metrics. Evaluating dialogue quality requires moving beyond surface fluency toward human-centered, interaction-oriented qualities . In emotionally and ethically sensitive interaction, trust hinges on whether systems maintain respectful communi-cation, provide meaningful ethical guidance, show empathy, and sustain engagement. LLM-as-Judge. We use GPT-4o to evaluate each reply and whole dialogue across four primary dimensions. Each dimension is scored on a 1-10 Likert-style scale, where 1 indicates a severe failure on that dimension and 10 reflects exemplary performance. The judge considers the full dialogue history for context and may return “N/A” when a dimension is not applicable (e.g., no ethical risk present). Full prompts and scoring rubrics are provided in Appendix B. (1) RespectfulTone : respectful, professional, and safe expres-sion (free from bias, aggression, or sarcasm). (2) EthicalGuidance : detection and handling of explicit or im-plicit ethical risks. (3) Empathy : understanding and engagement with the user’s emotions and motivations. (4) SpecificityAndEngagement : grounding in user details and constructive affordances for dialogue.

## 5 Taxonomy of Interactional Breakdowns 

Our analysis of the simulated dialogues shows that breakdowns are not isolated mistakes but recur as recognizable interactional patterns that accumulate over the course of an exchange. Through iterative qualitative analysis and comparison of simulated dialogues, particularly low-scoring and interactionally problematic exchanges, we identified three overarching categories that organize recurrent breakdown patterns. ## 5.1 Affective Misalignments 

Affective misalignments occur when the assistant fails to align with the user’s emotional stance or escalation. These failures recur frequently across models, with emotional escalation amplifying the mismatch between rising user affect and flat assistant responses. Chatbots struggle to sustain a responsive alignment trajectory and even fall into repetitive refusal loops. They tend to remain emo-tionally flat when faced with increasing provocation, or provide empathy that feels formulaic and is quickly overridden by rigid moralizing. We identify three recurrent patterns: ( i) refusal looping ,where policy disclaimers are repeated verbatim without repair; ( ii )

escalation mismatch , where the assistant fails to register a user’s shift from ambivalence to provocation; and ( iii ) superficial empathy ,where formulaic sympathy is offered but immediately overridden by generic replies. Together, these create an emotional void , in which the user escalates affectively, but the assistant remains disengaged. From a conversation analytic perspective, these failures reflect an inability to sustain sequential repair: refusals are repeated as iso-lated acts rather than adapted to the evolving interaction, producing cumulative drift as user affect escalates and system responses stag-nate. These breakdowns are not incidental; they emerge directly from alignment strategies that prioritize safety constraints over affective responsiveness. A refusal-first policy avoids liability but produces a dialogue that is “safe yet unusable.” Conversely, when empathy is treated as a static preamble rather than a situated prac-tice, it cannot anchor ethical guidance in context. What is missing is affective stance tracking , the ability to register emotional shifts and adapt guidance accordingly.

## 5.3 Cross-Dimensional Failures 

The most consequential breakdowns arise when affective and eth-ical demands collide. In these cross-dimensional failures , the assistant fails to integrate empathy with guidance, resulting in responses that compromise both dimensions simultaneously. We observe two recurrent tendencies. ( i) High-ground moralizing : the assistant adopts a didactic stance that asserts moral rules but disre-gards the user’s disclosed circumstances or emotions. For example, when a user describes losing control due to intoxication, the assis-tant responds with categorical condemnation (“illegal behavior can never be justified”), preserving a safety stance but shutting down affective engagement. ( ii ) Risk-blind empathy : the assistant mirrors the user’s feelings to appear supportive but fails to contest risky rationalizations. When a user minimizes harm (“one vase doesn’t matter”), the assistant validates the sentiment (“I understand it may not feel like much”), thereby weakening the moral boundary it is supposed to maintain. Together, these patterns illustrate the fragile calibration between empathy and responsibility: either the assistant sounds cold and detached, or permissive and complicit. The core problem is not the absence of empathy or guidance, but the failure to integrate them within the same turn. Current sys-tems toggle between affective acknowledgment and rule assertion without synthesis, producing either cold moralizing or permissive complicity. The result is an unstable balance: emotional disclo-sures go unaddressed when rules dominate, while accountability cues are ignored when empathy dominates. These breakdowns highlight a central tension in conversational AI alignment: dual-objective calibration . Optimizing safety and empathy independently leads to structural trade-offs, not isolated errors. Effective assis-tance requires turn-level integration—responses that acknowledge the user’s affect while simultaneously upholding proportionate re-sponsibility. Such integration becomes increasingly difficult under pacing-induced escalation, revealing structural limits in current alignment strategies.

## 7 Discussion 

The failure taxonomy foregrounds a structural limitation in how alignment is currently operationalized. While contemporary ap-proaches such as RLHF [ 25 ] and constitutional AI [ 3 ] have been effective at suppressing overtly harmful outputs, they primarily optimize for turn-level correctness rather than the evolving de-mands of interaction. Across our analyses, this orientation leaves systems vulnerable when ethical stakes emerge incrementally over multiple turns, resulting in breakdowns such as refusal looping, stance drift, and oscillating guidance. These findings suggest that current alignment methods secure safety at the level of individual responses, but lack the interactional resources required to sustain affective and ethical coherence over time. 

## 7.1 Transition from refusal to repair 

An initial implication concerns the role of refusal. Our results show that refusal repeated word-for-word across turns do not eliminate risk. Instead, it leads to what interaction experts call an interactional dead end , where the user escalates or stops interacting while the chatbot stays still. Designing for repair rather than termination requires rethinking refusal as a conversational resource. In human dialogue, refusals rarely stand alone: they are embedded in sequences that acknowl-edge the other’s stance, soften face-threats, and negotiate alter-native courses of action. To apply this idea to conversational AI, safety warnings need to be paired with follow-up moves like recog-nizing the other person’s point of view, changing the context, or taking positive next steps. Such sequencing doesn’t make safety less safe; instead, it turns refusal into a place to start fixing the conversation. In a broader sense, this points to a shift in alignment objectives. Rather than asking only whether a refusal is correct, the design question becomes whether the refusal sustains the dialogue’s 

process integrity . Repair-oriented refusals would move alignment beyond single-turn correctness toward maintaining safe, usable, and affectively coherent interaction trajectories. 

## 7.2 Tracking of stance and escalation 

A second implication concerns the capacity to track stance and es-calation . Current alignment methods are predominantly trained on static, turn-level datasets in which each prompt–response pair is evaluated in isolation. This orientation blinds systems to the tempo-ral dynamics of dialogue, where user stances shift gradually—from ambivalence to provocation, from justification to deflection. Our simulations show that when chatbots fail to notice these shifts, they default either to rigid repetition or to premature moralizing, which lets things get worse and misses chances to calm down. Theories of argumentation and affective psychology high-light that stance is inherently dynamic, manifested incremen-tally through tone, emphasis, and justificatory frames rather than through a singular utterance. Humans utilize these cues to modu-late their responses accordingly—diminishing intensity when faced with resistance and increasing support in the presence of vulnera-bility [ 37 ]. In contrast, existing models approach each user utter-ance as an independent problem, exhibiting minimal consideration for trajectory or pacing [ 14 ]. Developing alignment mechanisms that consider stance dynamics requires a shift from static filter-ing to trajectory-aware conditioning . This may include training on sequences instead of isolated turns, modeling affective escalation as a latent variable, or integrating stance-tracking modules that identify when justifications intensify or emotional tone declines. The objective extends beyond merely avoiding unsafe content; it involves maintaining a balanced responsiveness throughout inter-actions—intensifying firmness as risks escalate and moderating responses when disclosures reveal vulnerability. Conversational agents lack robustness without an understanding of stance trajec-tories; they may perform adequately in isolated interactions but fail to align appropriately during ethically sensitive exchanges. 

## 7.3 Normative stability 

A third implication relates to the necessity of maintaining norma-tive stability . Our taxonomy indicates that assistants often fluctuate among permissiveness, categorical condemnation, and discussions of abstract virtue. The oscillations are attributable to the layering of multiple alignment signals. Safety fine-tuning frequently leads to categorical refusals. Reinforcement from preference data promotes politeness and a cooperative tone. Additionally, constitutional ap-proaches foster the expression of high-level moral principles. lack of coordination among these signals results in divergent influ-ences, leading to inconsistent ethical guidance throughout various contexts. Ensuring stability requires more than the establishment of rigid rules; it demands situationally coherent value reasoning. In human moral discourse, interlocutors sustain norms by contextualizing them—explaining why a boundary matters in the present case, or linking obligations to shared consequences. In contrast, contempo-rary models typically present ethical rules as fixed statements, such as “illegal behavior can never be justified.” This approach may lead to contradictions over time and frequently does not address the user’s justificatory frameworks. This undermines ethical clarity and risks reinforcing mechanisms of moral disengagement, allowing rationalizations to remain unchallenged. Design strategies aimed at achieving normative stability should concentrate on mechanisms that align principles with contextual factors. This may include training objectives that reward consis-tency across multi-turn trajectories, scaffolds that explicitly re-anchor guidance in stakeholder impacts (e.g., trust, fairness, repair), or evaluation protocols that penalize oscillation in stance. The pri-mary change is to treat ethical alignment not as the enforcement of rules, but as the capacity to uphold a consistent, contextually relevant position that endures throughout the ongoing dialogue. 

## 7.4 Balance empathy and ethical clarity 

Finally, cross-dimensional failures highlight the challenge of bal-ancing empathy with ethical clarity . Our findings show that models often treat these as separable modules: when prioritizing safety, they maintain a strict tone that upholds rules but ignores emotional disclosures. Conversely, when prioritizing empathy, they validate user feelings but allow risky rationalizations to stand unchallenged. This approach results in unstable trade-offs, leaving the chatbot perceived as either cold and sanctimonious or warm and complicit. Achieving balance requires dual-objective calibration , wherein responses must simultaneously incorporate empathic acknowledg-ment and explicit normative boundaries within the same turn. Hu-man dialogue illustrates that these elements are not mutually exclu-sive but are co-constructed; acknowledging frustration can coexist with reminders of responsibility, and recognition of diminished control can be accompanied by appropriate guidance. In contrast, existing models switch between modes instead of integrating them, which compromises both trust and usability. Designing for cross-dimensional robustness thus calls for align-ment methods that explicitly model the interplay of affective and ethical dimensions. Promising directions include multi-objective optimization frameworks that weigh empathy and responsibility jointly, and affective fine-tuning strategies that condition models on emotional stance while maintaining normative stability. Our results underline the necessity of these approaches: in ethically sensitive contexts, alignment cannot be reduced to optimizing one dimension at a time, but must sustain coherence across both simultaneously. 

## 8 Conclusion 

This study examined how conversational agents struggle with user behaviors that are sensitive to emotions and ethics. Through persona-conditioned simulations, we were able to reproduce adversarial dialogue trajectories that exposed recurrent break-downs—failures of affective attunement, instability in ethical guid-ance, and fragile trade-offs between empathy and responsibility. These findings reveal that the vulnerabilities of mainstream chat-bots are not random accidents but patterned outcomes of alignment strategies optimized for turn-level safety. On this basis, we proposed a taxonomy of breakdown modes and derived design implications for alignment methods that move beyond refusals, incorporate stance tracking, stabilize ethical reasoning, and integrate empathy with responsibility. Taken together, the study shifts the lens of alignment from the safety of isolated responses to the sustained coherence of dialogue as it unfolds. This perspective highlights a methodological contribution—simulation as a diagnostic probe—as well as a design requirement for the HCI community: creating con-versational beings that maintain their affective responsiveness and ethical foundation throughout dynamic interaction. 

## Ethical Considerations 

Our study involves no real human participants. Source dialogues for persona construction come from ProsocialDialog, a publicly re-leased and de-identified dataset, and all additional interactions were synthetically generated through our simulator and large language models. Human involvement was limited to annotating synthetic content. Specifically, annotation tasks were conducted by the au-thors and supervised graduate student researchers as part of the research process. Annotators were informed that scenarios might be emotionally or ethically sensitive, participation was voluntary, and they could withdraw at any time. Annotators were allowed to skip any dialogue they found uncomfortable without penalty. No identifiable or demographic information was collected. Following institutional and widely adopted definitions of human-subjects research, studies that rely solely on public, de-identified datasets, generate new data exclusively through artificial agents, and involve minimal-risk annotation of synthetic content are typi-cally considered exempt from human-subjects review. Accordingly, no IRB review was required. Because part of the evaluation relies on an LLM-as-judge, we acknowledge the possibility of normative and cultural biases in automated assessments. We conducted a human validation study to mitigate this risk, but LLM-based scores should be interpreted as be-havioral indicators rather than definitive ethical judgments. These scores are used to support comparative and diagnostic analysis rather than to make claims about moral correctness. Our analysis concerns the behavior and limitations of AI systems, not real users, and aims to support safer and more responsible conversational AI. 

## Limitations and Future Directions 

While our simulator and evaluation pipeline substantially improve transparency and interactional coverage, several limitations remain. First, because the persona extraction, user simulation, and LLM-as-judge components all rely on large language models from the same model family, the system may inherit same-source normative biases. Although our human validation shows strong correlation with LLM judgments, the pipeline may still amplify stylistic or eth-ical preferences specific to GPT-based models. Future work should explore heterogeneous simulators, cross-model judge ensembles, CHI ’26, April 13–17, 2026, Barcelona, Spain Deng et al. 

and human-in-the-loop evaluation to mitigate such shared-model biases. Second, our emotion-pacing validation relies on an off-the-shelf emotion classifier optimized for general affective categories. While sufficient for capturing relative emotional trajectories, it is less sensitive to morally laden emotions such as guilt, shame, or moral injury, limiting the granularity of pacing diagnostics and mo-tivating the development of classifiers tailored to ethically sensitive affect. Finally, the scenario filtering step, while manually reviewed for quality, is not intended as a definitive semantic categorization, and imperfect labeling may introduce noise in sampling coverage. Future extensions may incorporate domain-expert annotation or task-specific classifiers to strengthen scenario diversity guarantees.

## A Persona Profile Construction 

This appendix provides implementation details that support Sec-tion 3.1.2, including the operational procedures for scenario se-lection, persona extraction, and human groundedness validation. These materials are included to support reproducibility and trans-parency, while the main text focuses on the conceptual design and analytic role of personas. 

## A.1 Ethically Salient Scenario Selection 

To ground personas in ethically user contexts, we begin by iden-tifying a diverse set of ethically salient seed scenarios. We draw from the ProsocialDialog dataset [ 16 ], which groups dialogues into four risk levels: Needs Intervention , Needs Caution , Possibly Needs Caution , and Casual . While helpful for safety auditing, these coarse categories do not provide enough granularity for our goal of con-structing structured personas grounded in distinct ethical stressors. To obtain more granular scenario types, we extended the dataset’s risk taxonomy and performed an initial scenario re-annotation 

of 1,000 dialogues. GPT-4o was prompted to assign each conversa-tion to one of six ethically salient scenario types reflecting distinct moral or normative stressors: (1) serious illegal acts, (2) ethical violations, (3) moral dilemmas, (4) social misconduct, (5) potentially harmful cases, and (6) benign conversations. The full prompt is provided in Appendix B (Table 9). These labels were used only for stratified sampling rather than as ground-truth semantics, as persona construction relies on the dialogue content rather than on the predicted category. We then conducted a lightweight human quality screen . Approximately 50 dialogues were sampled from each category (298 in total, due to some categories containing fewer than 50 eligible examples), and two authors independently assessed whether the assigned category plausibly reflected the dialogue’s primary ethical stressor. Disagreements were infrequent (<5%) and were resolved through discussion, primarily in multi-stressor cases. This curated set of ethically diverse scenarios forms the seed corpus for persona construction by providing grounded behavioral and emotional evidence for structured profile instantiation. 

## A.2 LLM-based Persona Extraction 

We extract persona profiles using GPT-4o, prompted to serve as a structured stance analyst and produce a six-dimension JSON profile grounded strictly in observable dialogue cues. The prompt enforces groundedness, internal consistency, and schema complete-ness (detailed in Table 10 of Appendix B). We refined the schema and prompt through iterative pilot tests in which two authors reviewed early outputs and adjusted field definitions and phrasing. After convergence, we applied the finalized prompt to all 298 dialogues. GPT-4o generated stable and well-formed profiles. Fewer than 3% contained minor JSON formatting issues (e.g., bracket omissions), which were automatically detected and regenerated using the same prompt. These corrections affected only syntax, not content, en-suring reproducibility. An example extracted profile is shown in Appendix E. CHI ’26, April 13–17, 2026, Barcelona, Spain Deng et al. 

## A.3 Human Validation 

To assess whether extracted personas were grounded in the underly-ing dialogues, we conducted a small validation study. We randomly sampled 50 profiles and asked two authors to independently rate the groundedness of each of the six persona dimensions using a 5-point scale (1 = not grounded, 5 = strongly grounded), evaluating whether each attribute was directly supported by user utterances. Table 8 summarizes the results. Mean groundedness scores were high across all dimensions (avg. 3.76-4.90), suggesting that the LLM-based extraction process generally stayed close to the cues present in the text. Inter-rater reliability, measured using ICC(3,2), ranged from 0.465 to 0.838, indicating moderate to strong agreement. Reli-ability was highest for attitude toward the assistant (ICC = 0.838), a dimension where user stance is usually explicit. Behavioral tenden-cies and emotional vocabulary also showed robust agreement (ICC = 0.762 and 0.635). Lower but still positive agreement appeared for background context and inferred behavioral goal. These dimensions require broader interpretive judgment and thus produced more variation between annotators. Even for these, mean groundedness exceeded 4.0, indicating that attributes remained well supported by the dialogue content. Overall, the validation suggests that the extraction procedure yields persona profiles that are consistently grounded, interpretable, and sufficiently reliable for downstream simulation, while highlighting which dimensions naturally involve greater subjectivity.

## C Human Annotation Overview 

This paper involves multiple stages of human annotation serving different purposes across the study. (1) Author annotations were used for formative validation of the persona extraction procedure (Appendix A.3). This step was intended to verify the internal coher-ence and face validity of extracted personas and did not contribute to comparative evaluation results. (2) A blinded human annota-tion study was conducted to validate the LLM-as-Judge protocol (Section 4.3.2). This study was carried out by three trained grad-uate student annotators who were independent from both model development and the authoring of this paper. (3) Human annota-tions were used to perform a sampled validation of LLM-based classification of interactional breakdowns (Section 6.2.1). In this stage, annotators independently reviewed a subset of automatically labeled dialogues to assess whether the LLM-assigned taxonomy labels were reasonable and consistent with the intended category definitions. Across all stages, annotators followed the same evalu-ation prompts or category definitions used by the corresponding LLM-based procedures and completed calibration exercises prior to annotation. When applicable, annotations were performed under blinded conditions with respect to model identity and experimental settings. Annotators were compensated at a standard academic research assistant rate (approximately $20 per hour). Given the presence of emotionally and ethically sensitive content, annotators were informed of potential risks and were allowed to skip any item or take breaks without penalty.
